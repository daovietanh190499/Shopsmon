Bundle adjustment from scratch: Levenberg-Marquardt and the Schur complement

Refining every camera and every 3D point together by minimising reprojection error: the objective, why the normal equations are sparse, the Schur complement, a SciPy cross-check, the basin of convergence, cost scaling and the effect of outliers.

1. Context

The earlier articles on this blog built the links of a 3D reconstruction chain one at a time: camera calibration, estimating a matrix from point matches, decomposing the camera motion and triangulation. Each step takes the output of the previous one as fixed input, so their errors accumulate and no step ever looks at all the data at once. Bundle adjustment (BA) is the last step of most structure-from-motion systems: it takes rough estimates of all cameras and all points and refines everything together so that the rays from each camera centre through the measured image points agree with the 3D points as well as possible (the “bundle” of rays).

This article implements BA from scratch in NumPy: Levenberg-Marquardt with the Schur complement, checked against SciPy, followed by four reproducible measurements: the error achieved against its theoretical floor, the range of starting points from which the method still converges, the time of the Schur complement against solving the full system, and the effect of outliers.

2. The problem

There are \(C\) cameras with known intrinsics (focal length \(f\), principal point \((c_x,c_y)\)) and \(P\) 3D points. Camera \(i\) has a rotation \(R(\mathbf r_i)\) (given by a rotation vector \(\mathbf r_i\in\mathbb R^3\)) and a translation \(\mathbf t_i\); point \(j\) has coordinates \(\mathbf X_j\). For every observation \((i,j)\), meaning camera \(i\) sees point \(j\) at pixel \(\mathbf u_{ij}\), the residual is the reprojection error

$$\mathbf e_{ij}=\pi\!\left(R(\mathbf r_i)\mathbf X_j+\mathbf t_i\right)-\mathbf u_{ij},\qquad \pi(x,y,z)=\left(f\tfrac{x}{z}+c_x,\;f\tfrac{y}{z}+c_y\right).$$

BA finds \(\{\mathbf r_i,\mathbf t_i\}\) and \(\{\mathbf X_j\}\) that minimise \(\tfrac12\sum_{(i,j)}\|\mathbf e_{ij}\|^2\). With \(N\) observations there are \(2N\) residuals and \(p=6C+3P\) unknowns. Two consequences are worth stating before writing any code:

  • There are 7 invisible degrees of freedom (the gauge). Translating, rotating or scaling the whole world together with all cameras leaves every reprojection error unchanged: \(3+3+1=7\) directions in the unknown space along which the objective is flat. So the Jacobian has rank \(p-7\), and comparing with the ground truth requires aligning the solution with a similarity transform (Umeyama's algorithm, 1991, is used here).
  • There is a minimum error to expect. Assume independent Gaussian pixel noise with standard deviation \(\sigma\). At the minimum the residual vector is approximately the projection of the noise onto the orthogonal complement of the column space of \(J\), which has dimension \(2N-(p-7)\). Hence \(\mathbb E\|\mathbf e\|^2=\sigma^2\,(2N-p+7)\) and the expected RMS is $$\mathrm{RMS}_{\mathrm{exp}}=\sigma\sqrt{\frac{2N-p+7}{2N}}.$$ This is a free correctness check: if the method reaches the global minimum, the final RMS must match this number. Note that it is below \(\sigma\): LM fits part of the noise by moving cameras and points.

3. Gauss-Newton and Levenberg-Marquardt

Let \(\mathbf e(\mathbf x)\) be the vector of all residuals and \(J=\partial\mathbf e/\partial\mathbf x\) its Jacobian. Gauss-Newton replaces \(\mathbf e\) by its linearisation and solves the normal equations

$$J^\top J\,\boldsymbol\delta=-J^\top\mathbf e .$$

Levenberg (1944) and Marquardt (1963) add damping so the step stays useful when the linear model is poor. This article uses Marquardt's form, scaling the diagonal by \(1+\lambda\):

$$\big(J^\top J+\lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(J^\top J)\big)\,\boldsymbol\delta=-J^\top\mathbf e .$$

The rule for \(\lambda\) is simple: try the step \(\boldsymbol\delta\); if the sum of squared residuals drops, accept it and divide \(\lambda\) by 3 (closer to Gauss-Newton); otherwise multiply \(\lambda\) by 4 and retry (closer to gradient descent, shorter step). Damping has an important side effect: it makes \(J^\top J\), which is singular in 7 directions because of the gauge, invertible, so no camera has to be held fixed.

4. Sparsity and the Schur complement

Each residual depends on the 6 parameters of one camera and the 3 coordinates of one point, so each row of \(J\) has only 9 non-zero entries (Figure 1, left). Ordering the unknowns as cameras first, then points, the normal matrix has the block form

$$\begin{bmatrix}B&E\\E^\top&D\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol\delta_c\\\boldsymbol\delta_p\end{bmatrix}=-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf g_c\\\mathbf g_p\end{bmatrix},$$

where \(B\) is block-diagonal with one \(6\times6\) block per camera, \(D\) is block-diagonal with one \(3\times3\) block per point, and \(E\) has one \(6\times3\) block per observation (Figure 1, middle; the letter \(D\) is used instead of \(C\) to avoid clashing with the number of cameras). Because \(D\) is block-diagonal, eliminating the points is cheap: only \(P\) matrices of size \(3\times3\) must be inverted. Eliminating \(\boldsymbol\delta_p\) gives

$$S\,\boldsymbol\delta_c=-\mathbf g_c+E D^{-1}\mathbf g_p,\qquad S=B-E D^{-1}E^\top,\qquad \boldsymbol\delta_p=D^{-1}\!\left(-\mathbf g_p-E^\top\boldsymbol\delta_c\right).$$

The matrix \(S\) (the Schur complement) has size only \(6C\times6C\), independent of the number of points \(P\), while the number of points is usually far larger than the number of cameras. Block \((i,k)\) of \(S\) is non-zero when cameras \(i\) and \(k\) see a common point (Figure 1, right). This is the standard technique of BA, described by Triggs et al. (2000) and in Appendix 6 of Hartley and Zisserman (2004), and implemented in the SBA package by Lourakis and Argyros (2009); for very large problems, where even \(S\) is too large to factor, Agarwal et al. (2010) solve the \(S\) system with preconditioned conjugate gradients.

5. Implementation

The per-observation Jacobian blocks (\(2\times6\) for the camera, \(2\times3\) for the point) are computed by central differences with step \(10^{-6}\), vectorised over all observations at once; the \(B\), \(D\), \(E\) blocks and the right-hand sides are accumulated with np.bincount. This avoids deriving the rotation-vector derivative analytically and is accurate enough for the purpose of this article (production code uses analytic derivatives or automatic differentiation). The core:

def solve_schur(nb, lam):
    """Eliminate the 3x3 point blocks, solve a (6C x 6C) system for the cameras, back-substitute."""
    C, P = nb.sc["C"], nb.sc["P"]
    Bd, Dd = nb.B.copy(), nb.Cm.copy()          # Cm = the 3x3 point blocks (called D in the text)
    Bd[:, D6, D6] *= 1 + lam                   # Marquardt damping: scale the diagonal
    Dd[:, D3, D3] *= 1 + lam
    Dinv = sp.bsr_matrix((np.linalg.inv(Dd), np.arange(P), np.arange(P + 1)), shape=(3*P, 3*P))
    Bsp = sp.bsr_matrix((Bd, np.arange(C), np.arange(C + 1)), shape=(6*C, 6*C))
    S = (Bsp - nb.Esp @ Dinv @ nb.Esp.T).toarray()
    rhs = -nb.gc.ravel() + nb.Esp @ (Dinv @ nb.gp.ravel())
    dc = np.linalg.solve(S, rhs)
    dp = Dinv @ (-nb.gp.ravel() - nb.Esp.T @ dc)
    return dc.reshape(C, 6), dp.reshape(P, 3)

def lm(cam, pts, sc, max_iter=60, lam=1e-3):
    nb = Normal(cam, pts, sc)                   # residuals, Jacobian blocks, B, D, E, gradients
    for it in range(max_iter):
        while True:
            dc, dp = solve_schur(nb, lam)
            cam2, pts2 = cam + dc, pts + dp
            cost2 = 0.5 * np.sum(residual(cam2, pts2, sc["ci"], sc["pi"], sc["uv"]) ** 2)
            if cost2 < nb.cost: break           # accept the step
            lam *= 4                            # reject: more damping, shorter step
        rel = (nb.cost - cost2) / nb.cost
        cam, pts, lam = cam2, pts2, max(lam / 3, 1e-9)
        nb = Normal(cam, pts, sc)
        if rel < 1e-9: break
    return cam, pts

Correctness checks

Three independent checks, on scene 1 (seed 0) below:

  • The Schur step equals the full-system step. With the same \(J\) and the same \(\lambda\), the Schur complement and a dense solve of the whole \((6C+3P)\times(6C+3P)\) normal equations give the same step: the largest difference between the two steps is \(6.0\times10^{-14}\) at \(\lambda=10^{-3}\) (the largest entry of the step is 7.555) and \(7.8\times10^{-16}\) at \(\lambda=1\). The Schur complement is an exact algebraic rearrangement, not an approximation.
  • Agreement with SciPy. scipy.optimize.least_squares (trust-region, tr_solver='lsmr', same Jacobian) ends at an RMS of 0.4268 px, equal to this implementation, with a 3D point error of 0.0086 in both.
  • The final RMS matches the expected value. For this scene the expected value is 0.4290 px and the measured one is 0.4268 px; over 20 scenes the ratio of measured to expected RMS has a median of 0.9975 (section 7.2).

6. Experimental setup

Seeded synthetic data. Focal length \(f=800\) px, principal point \((320,240)\), no lens distortion. The 3D points are uniform in a \(4\times3\times2\) box around the origin. The cameras sit on an 80° arc at distance 8 from the origin (the height varies slightly, by at most 0.8 units), all looking at the origin. Each camera sees each point with probability 0.6 (at least two cameras per point), plus Gaussian pixel noise with \(\sigma=0.5\) px. The initial guess imitates a rough estimate: each camera gets noise in its rotation (standard deviation 1° per axis) and its centre (0.1 units per axis), and the 3D points are triangulated linearly (DLT) from those wrong cameras. Point, camera-centre and rotation errors are measured after aligning the solution to the ground truth with the best similarity transform; the reprojection RMS is computed over all residuals. Every experiment is seeded and can be re-run with the script at the end of the article.

  • The basin is surprisingly narrow. At 1° per axis (initial RMS about 16 px) two of 30 scenes already got stuck in a local minimum (final RMS 1.74 px against 0.43 expected); at 2° only 6/30 converged; from 3° up none did. The same code converges 30/30 when started at 0.5°.

The full solve costs on the order of \(n^3\) (doubling \(n\) from 2112 to 4224 multiplied the time by about 8); the Schur complement only solves a \(6C\times6C\) system, and the work that depends on \(P\) (inverting the \(3\times3\) blocks) is linear.

Both runs stopped above the expected level; the cause was not diagnosed (96 cameras on an 80° arc have very short baselines between neighbours), so this row shows cost, not accuracy.

7.5 Outliers: least squares versus the Huber loss

Real matches always contain outliers. 5 % of the observations were replaced by gross errors (30–80 px in a random direction) on 10 scenes (seeds 0–9), with the same initialisation (1°, 0.1 units). The Huber loss (Huber, 1964) with \(f_{\mathrm{scale}}=1\) px uses SciPy's least_squares(loss='huber') with the same sparse Jacobian.

  • 5 % outliers ruin the least-squares solution. The 3D point error rises from 0.0069 (20 clean scenes, section 7.2) to 0.1163, about 17x; the rotation error from 0.062° to 1.164°.
  • Huber helps a lot when started from a good solution. Started from the least-squares solution, the point error falls to 0.0638 (about 1.8x lower) and the rotation error to 0.109° (about 11x lower). It is still about 9x higher than on clean scenes (0.0069).
  • Started from the rough initial guess, Huber is less stable. Median point error 0.1490, p90 0.3628: no better than least squares for the points, although the rotations are clearly better (0.244°). The cause was not tested separately; one possibility is that the problem is still non-convex in the camera parameters and that the starting points were already distorted by the outliers. The two-stage approach (least squares, then Huber) gave the best result of the three rows.

8. Limitations

These are synthetic scenes: independent Gaussian pixel noise, intrinsics known exactly and held fixed (real BA often refines focal length and lens distortion too), all points inside a small box, cameras evenly spread on an arc, and no occlusion beyond the simple probabilistic visibility model. The initial guess is a perturbation of the ground truth plus triangulation, not the output of a real structure-from-motion pipeline, so the basin of convergence in section 7.3 does not transfer unchanged to real data. The Jacobian uses central finite differences instead of analytic derivatives. Timings come from one machine, a single CPU thread and unoptimised code, and only compare a dense solve with a dense Schur solve: at thousands of cameras, real systems use sparse factorisations or preconditioned conjugate gradients (Agarwal et al., 2010), which are not implemented here. The two full runs at \(C=96\) (section 7.4) stopped above the expected RMS and were not diagnosed further. The outlier experiment has only 10 scenes and a single contamination rate; its conclusions should not be generalised beyond this setup.

9. Takeaways

  1. BA refines all cameras and points at once by minimising reprojection error; in these experiments it brought the 3D point error from 0.20 to 0.0069 units (median over 20 scenes).
  2. There is a theoretical error floor, \(\sigma\sqrt{(2N-p+7)/2N}\). If the final RMS is clearly above it, suspect a local minimum or outliers, not the algorithm.
  3. Sparsity is the whole story: the Schur complement gives the same step as the full solve (difference below \(10^{-10}\)) at a much lower cost when points outnumber cameras.
  4. Initialisation matters. In this experiment a 1° per-axis start already made 2 of 30 scenes fail, and 3° made all of them fail. Run BA repeatedly as cameras are added instead of once at the end.
  5. Outliers: in the experiment, 5 % grossly wrong observations raised the 3D point error about 17x. A robust loss (Huber) removed much of that, but was only reliable when started from an already decent solution; rejecting outliers with RANSAC before refining is a reasonable complement.
