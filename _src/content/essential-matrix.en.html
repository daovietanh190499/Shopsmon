From the essential matrix to camera motion: four solutions and one test

Decomposing \(E\) into rotation and translation, why there are exactly four candidates, how the cheirality test picks one, and what noise, baseline and the number of matches do to the result.

1. Context

Two earlier articles on this blog covered the pieces on either side of this one: the eight-point algorithm estimates a matrix from point matches, and triangulation turns two views plus two cameras into a 3D point. This article is the step in between. When the camera intrinsics \(K\) are known (for example after a chessboard calibration), the matrix you estimate is the essential matrix \(E\), and the question becomes: what motion of the camera does \(E\) encode?

The answer has a catch that trips up many first implementations: \(E\) does not give you one rotation and one translation. It gives you four, and the data that tells them apart is not in \(E\) at all. It is in the 3D points.

2. What the essential matrix is

Take the first camera as the world frame, so a point has coordinates \(\mathbf X\) in camera 1 and \(\mathbf X'=R\mathbf X+\mathbf t\) in camera 2. Work in normalised image coordinates, \(\hat{\mathbf x}=K^{-1}\mathbf x=(X/Z,\,Y/Z,\,1)^\top\), which removes the intrinsics. The three vectors \(\mathbf X'\), \(R\mathbf X\) and \(\mathbf t\) are coplanar (they lie in the epipolar plane), so the triple product vanishes:

$$\hat{\mathbf x}'^\top\,[\mathbf t]_\times R\,\hat{\mathbf x}=0,\qquad E=[\mathbf t]_\times R,\qquad [\mathbf t]_\times=\begin{bmatrix}0&-t_3&t_2\\ t_3&0&-t_1\\ -t_2&t_1&0\end{bmatrix}.$$

Two facts about \(E\) matter for what follows.

  • Its singular values are \((s,s,0)\). A cross-product matrix has rank 2 and two equal singular values \(\|\mathbf t\|\), and multiplying by a rotation does not change singular values. Huang and Faugeras (1989) proved the converse: any \(3\times3\) matrix with singular values \((s,s,0)\) can be written as a skew-symmetric matrix times a rotation.
  • Scale is lost. \(E\) is only defined up to a scalar, so \(\mathbf t\) is recovered only as a direction. With two images alone you cannot tell a small scene seen with a short baseline from a large one seen with a long baseline.

In practice \(E\) comes from the same eight-point system as before, built from normalised coordinates, with Hartley normalisation applied on top. The raw estimate will not have singular values \((s,s,0)\) because of noise, so the last step of estimation is to replace the singular values by \((s,s,0)\), with \(s\) the mean of the two largest. That is the closest valid essential matrix in the Frobenius sense (see Hartley & Zisserman, Section 9.6, in the references).

3. Decomposition: why four

Let \(E=U\,\mathrm{diag}(1,1,0)\,V^\top\) be an SVD, with \(U,V\) rotations (flip the sign of a column if a determinant comes out as \(-1\); this is harmless because the third singular value is zero). Define

$$W=\begin{bmatrix}0&-1&0\\ 1&0&0\\ 0&0&1\end{bmatrix}.$$

Then the candidates are

$$R_1=UWV^\top,\qquad R_2=UW^\top V^\top,\qquad \mathbf t=\pm\mathbf u_3,$$

where \(\mathbf u_3\) is the third column of \(U\). Combining two rotations with two signs gives the four pairs \((R_1,+\mathbf t)\), \((R_1,-\mathbf t)\), \((R_2,+\mathbf t)\), \((R_2,-\mathbf t)\) (Hartley & Zisserman, Result 9.19). All four produce exactly the same \(E\), hence exactly the same epipolar geometry. No amount of image-plane arithmetic can prefer one.

The two signs of \(\mathbf t\) are the familiar ambiguity: the second camera is on one side of the first, or the other (and, equivalently, the scene is mirrored through the first camera centre). The two rotations are the less obvious part. Compute their ratio:

$$R_2R_1^\top=U\,(W^\top)^2\,U^\top=U\,\mathrm{diag}(-1,-1,1)\,U^\top .$$

That is a rotation by 180° about \(\mathbf u_3\), which is the baseline direction. This is the “twisted pair” of Hartley & Zisserman: the second camera is flipped over around the line joining the two camera centres. Both views would still agree on the epipolar constraint, because a rotation about the baseline maps every epipolar plane to itself.

4. Choosing one: the cheirality test

What separates the four is physical: a real scene point must be in front of both cameras, that is, have positive depth in each. This is called the cheirality constraint (Hartley, 1998, uses it under the name “chirality”). The procedure:

  1. For each of the four candidates, build \(P=[I\mid\mathbf 0]\) and \(P'=[R\mid\mathbf t]\) and triangulate every match.
  2. Count the points with positive depth in camera 1 and in camera 2.
  3. Keep the candidate with the highest count. With clean data, exactly one candidate has all points in front; the other three have none, or a mix.

Using all matches, not a single one, makes the test robust: one badly matched point can fall behind a camera for the right answer, but it will not flip the majority.

5. Implementation

The core, in NumPy. The complete script, including the scene generator, the experiments and the figures, is downloadable.

import numpy as np

W = np.array([[0.0, -1, 0], [1, 0, 0], [0, 0, 1]])

def decompose(E):
    """The four candidate (R, t) of an essential matrix; |t| = 1."""
    U, _, Vt = np.linalg.svd(E)
    if np.linalg.det(U) < 0:  U[:, 2] *= -1     # make U, V proper rotations
    if np.linalg.det(Vt) < 0: Vt[2] *= -1
    R1 = U @ W @ Vt
    R2 = U @ W.T @ Vt
    t = U[:, 2]
    return [(R1, t), (R1, -t), (R2, t), (R2, -t)]

def count_in_front(R, t, n1, n2):
    """Cheirality: points with positive depth in BOTH cameras (n1, n2: normalised coords)."""
    X = triangulate(R, t, n1, n2)            # linear DLT, P1 = [I|0], P2 = [R|t]
    z1 = X[:, 2]
    z2 = (X @ R.T + t)[:, 2]
    return int(np.sum((z1 > 0) & (z2 > 0)))

def recover_pose(n1, n2):
    E = estimate_E(n1, n2)                   # normalised 8-point + (s, s, 0) projection
    cands = decompose(E)
    counts = [count_in_front(R, t, n1, n2) for R, t in cands]
    return cands[int(np.argmax(counts))]

Sanity checks

With noise-free matches (seed 0, 50 points) the recovered pose is exact up to rounding: rotation error 1.7×10-6 degrees, translation-direction error 0, and 50 of 50 points in front. The tiny non-zero rotation figure is most likely the arccos in the angle formula amplifying round-off of order 10-16 to about 10-8 radians; I did not investigate further because it is five orders of magnitude below any noise effect measured below.

The estimated \(E\) in the noisy demo below has singular values \((1,1,0)\) after normalisation, as the projection step forces.

7. Experiment: noise, baseline, number of matches

The demo is one scene. For the rest I ran 200 independent seeded scenes per row (random points, rotation angle of 7.5–15°, random baseline direction), 50 matches unless stated, and report the median error with the 10th and 90th percentiles. “Correct branch” means the candidate chosen by the cheirality test had rotation error below 5° and translation-direction error below 30°, so it is the right one of the four and not merely close in rotation.

  • Rotation does not care about the baseline. Its median error stays between 0.38° and 0.57° from baseline 0 (a camera that only rotates) to baseline 2.
  • The translation direction does. At baseline 0.02 (about 0.3% of the typical scene depth of 6) the median direction error is 74°, no better than a guess, and only 11.5% of scenes pick the right branch. At 0.25 the median is 7.9°; at 1.0 it is 1.5°. This is the same phenomenon as in the triangulation article, seen from the other side: with a tiny baseline there is almost no parallax, and the parallax is the only information about \(\mathbf t\).
  • Practical consequence: when the camera mostly rotates (a handheld pan, a gimbal) the rotation from \(E\) is still useful, while the translation and the 3D points are not. Systems that deal with such motion typically fit a homography or a pure-rotation model instead and switch to \(E\) only once there is enough parallax.

8. Limitations

These are synthetic scenes with isotropic Gaussian pixel noise, exact intrinsics \(K\), no lens distortion and no outlier matches. Real feature matches contain outliers, which the linear eight-point estimate does not tolerate at all; in practice the estimation is wrapped in RANSAC. Nistér’s (2004) five-point algorithm is the usual minimal solver for this, because it needs only five matches per hypothesis and so needs far fewer RANSAC iterations than eight. Errors in \(K\) will add to the figures above. The points were placed 4–8 units from the camera, so the baseline numbers describe a baseline-to-depth ratio, not absolute units. I measured pose errors only; I did not test degenerate scene configurations such as all points lying on a plane, where the eight-point estimate is itself ill-defined.

9. Practical takeaways

  1. \(E\) gives four \((R,\mathbf t)\) candidates, never one: two rotations that differ by 180° about the baseline, and two signs of \(\mathbf t\). Test all four.
  2. Use cheirality on all matches (positive depth in both cameras), not on a single point.
  3. Translation is a direction only. The scale must come from somewhere else: a known distance, an IMU, a stereo rig.
  4. If the baseline is small compared to the scene depth, trust the rotation and distrust the translation. In the experiment above, a baseline of about 0.3% of the depth gave a median translation error of 74°.
  5. Use well over eight matches (at least 20 in the experiment above) and wrap the estimation in RANSAC for real data.
