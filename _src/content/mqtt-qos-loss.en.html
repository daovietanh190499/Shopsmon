MQTT QoS 0, 1 and 2 on a lossy link: closed forms, simulation and burst loss

A sensor node on a weak radio link has to choose a delivery guarantee: send once and hope (QoS 0), retry until acknowledged (QoS 1), or run a four-packet handshake for exactly-once delivery (QoS 2). This post derives what each level delivers, what it costs in packets and delay and how often the sender is told “failed” although the message arrived, checks every formula by simulation, and then shows what happens when losses come in bursts instead of independently.

1. Background and scope

MQTT defines three quality-of-service levels for a PUBLISH: QoS 0 (at most once), QoS 1 (at least once, acknowledged by PUBACK) and QoS 2 (exactly once, PUBLISH/PUBREC/PUBREL/PUBCOMP). A common mental model is “the client retransmits until the broker acknowledges”. That is only true for some transports. In MQTT 3.1.1 (OASIS, 2014, section 4.4) the only case where a client or server is required to redeliver is a reconnect with CleanSession = 0; MQTT 5.0 (OASIS, 2019, section 4.4) goes further and says clients and servers must not resend at any other time. The reason is that MQTT normally runs over TCP, which already retransmits lost segments; the specification's non-normative note says retransmission at the MQTT layer was historically needed on some older TCP networks.

The picture is different on UDP-style links. MQTT-SN, the variant for sensor networks (Stanford-Clark and Truong, IBM, version 1.2, 2013), keeps the same QoS levels but section 6.13 specifies a retry timer \(T_\text{retry}\) and a retry counter \(N_\text{retry}\): every unicast message that expects a reply is retransmitted when the timer expires, and after \(N_\text{retry}\) retransmissions the client assumes its connection to the gateway is lost. Section 7.2 lists best-practice values of 10–15 s for \(T_\text{retry}\) and 3–5 for \(N_\text{retry}\). This post models exactly that behaviour on a single lossy hop. It is not a model of MQTT over TCP, where the loss that matters is a connection drop, not a single packet.

2. Model and metrics

A sender transmits one message to a receiver over a link. Every packet, in either direction, is lost with probability \(p\), independently (Sections 3–8; Section 9 relaxes this). The sender is stop-and-wait: it sends, waits \(T_\text{retry}\), and retransmits (same message id, DUP = 1) at most \(N=3\) times, so there are \(A=N+1=4\) attempts.

  • QoS 0: one PUBLISH, no reply.
  • QoS 1: PUBLISH, then PUBACK. The receiver hands the message to the application on every PUBLISH it receives, duplicates included, and answers each with a PUBACK.
  • QoS 2: PUBLISH, then PUBREC; then PUBREL, then PUBCOMP. The receiver hands the message over once, on the first PUBLISH (one of the two methods the specification allows), and answers every PUBLISH with a PUBREC and every PUBREL with a PUBCOMP. The second phase starts only if the first was confirmed.

Metrics, per message: the probability that no PUBLISH ever reaches the receiver (never delivered); the probability that the sender gives up without the reply (unconfirmed for QoS 1, incomplete for QoS 2); the number of times the application receives the message; the number of packets on the link in both directions; and the time until the sender sees the PUBACK.

3. Closed forms for independent losses

An exchange (a PUBLISH and its reply) succeeds with probability \(q=(1-p)^2\). Write \(A=N+1\).

  • Never delivered (QoS 1 and 2): all \(A\) PUBLISH packets are lost, \(p^A\).
  • QoS 1 unconfirmed: every attempt's exchange fails, \((1-q)^A\).
  • Expected PUBLISH transmissions: \(E[T]=\sum_{k=0}^{A-1}(1-q)^k=\dfrac{1-(1-q)^A}{q}\).
  • Expected application deliveries in QoS 1: \((1-p)\,E[T]\). The event “attempt \(k\) is sent” depends only on earlier attempts, and attempt \(k\)'s PUBLISH arrives independently with probability \(1-p\), so Wald's identity applies.
  • Packets in QoS 1: PUBLISH packets plus one PUBACK per received PUBLISH, \((2-p)\,E[T]\).
  • QoS 2: phase 1 is confirmed with probability \(1-(1-q)^A\); the sender is incomplete with probability \(1-\bigl(1-(1-q)^A\bigr)^2\); expected packets \((2-p)\,E[T]\,\bigl(1+1-(1-q)^A\bigr)\).
  • Unconfirmed but delivered (QoS 1): \((1-q)^A-p^A\), the sender reports failure although the receiver has the message.

4. Implementation

The full script (closed forms, simulation, the burst channel with an exact check, three figures; about 20 seconds) can be downloaded. The core of the QoS 1 simulation, vectorised over messages:

A = 4                                            # 1 transmission + 3 retransmissions
pub = rng.random((n, A)) >= p                    # PUBLISH of attempt k arrives
rep = rng.random((n, A)) >= p                    # its PUBACK arrives
ok = pub & rep                                   # whole exchange succeeds
confirmed = ok.any(1)
first = np.where(confirmed, ok.argmax(1), A - 1)  # index of the successful (or last) attempt
sent = np.arange(A)[None, :] < (first + 1)[:, None]
deliveries = (pub & sent).sum(1)                 # one application delivery per received PUBLISH
packets = (first + 1) + deliveries               # PUBLISH sent + PUBACK sent

\(N=3\) retransmissions (four attempts; MQTT-SN recommends 3–5), \(T_\text{retry}=10\) s (the lower end of the recommended range), one-way delay 50 ms (an assumption), 400,000 messages per row, fixed seed 20261121; the script prints the same numbers on every run (checked by running it twice and comparing the output). A consumer that adds each reading to a total (an energy counter, a pulse count) would overcount by 23% unless it deduplicates, for example on a sequence number carried in the payload.
  • QoS 2 removes duplicates at a price. By construction it hands over exactly one copy; the price at \(p=0.2\) is 5.49 packets per message against 2.77 for QoS 1 and 1 for QoS 0; across the table QoS 2 uses 1.7 to 2.0 times the packets of QoS 1.
  • Delay is set by the timer, not the link. One lost packet costs a full \(T_\text{retry}\): at \(p=0.2\) the mean time to confirmation is 5.06 s, fifty times the 0.1 s round trip.

The recommended \(N_\text{retry}\) of 3–5 (four to six attempts) therefore meets a 0.1% sender-failure target at 10% loss (5 attempts, 4 retransmissions) but not at 20% (7 attempts, 6 retransmissions); at \(p=0.3\) the same target needs 10 retransmissions, and with \(T_\text{retry}=10\) s the last one would leave 100 s after the first transmission.

9. Experiment 4: losses that come in bursts

Radio loss is rarely independent: interference, a person walking past or a door closing makes the link bad for a while. The classic two-state model is due to Gilbert (1960) and Elliott (1963). Here, per attempt the channel is Good (loss 0) or Bad (loss 0.8) with stationary probability 0.25 of Bad, so the mean loss is 0.25 × 0.8 = 0.20, the same as the \(p=0.20\) row above. Both packets of one exchange see the same state. Between consecutive attempts the state is kept with probability \(\rho\) and otherwise redrawn from the stationary law, so \(\rho\) is the correlation between consecutive attempts: \(\rho=0\) means every retry sees a fresh channel, \(\rho\to1\) means a burst longer than the whole retry sequence. The script computes each probability exactly (a two-state forward recursion) and by simulation (400,000 messages).

  • Across attempts, correlation hurts a lot. With the same mean loss, the probability that no PUBLISH ever arrives rises from 0.16% (independent) to 2.5% at \(\rho=0.5\) (15.6 times), 6.3% at \(\rho=0.8\) (39 times) and 10.0% at \(\rho=0.99\) (63 times). The limit is easy to read: if the whole retry sequence sits in one state, the message is lost with probability \(0.25\times0.8^4=0.1024\).
  • Inside one exchange, correlation helps. At \(\rho=0\) the never-delivered probability equals the independent case (0.00160, since attempts are independent with the same marginal loss), but the sender is unconfirmed five times less often (0.00332 against 0.01680): when the PUBLISH got through the link is probably good, so the PUBACK probably does too. An exchange succeeds with probability \(0.75+0.25\times0.2^2=0.76\) instead of \(0.8^2=0.64\). For \(\rho\ge0.5\) this advantage is lost: unconfirmed rises to 3.1 times (\(\rho=0.5\)) and 12.4 times (\(\rho=0.99\)) the independent value.
  • More retransmissions help little when bursts are longer than the retry spacing. At \(\rho=0.95\), going from 4 to 20 attempts lowers the never-delivered probability only from 9.13% to 0.139% (6 attempts: 5.41%; 10 attempts: 1.90%), while independent losses with 20 attempts reach \(10^{-14}\) (about \(6.4\times10^{-5}\) at 6 attempts and \(1.0\times10^{-7}\) at 10). The sender-unconfirmed probability at 20 attempts is 5.3% against \(1.3\times10^{-9}\) for independent losses.

10. Limitations

  • One hop, stop-and-wait, fixed \(T_\text{retry}\); no queueing, congestion, back-off, sleep cycles or gateway hop. A real MQTT-SN deployment also has a TCP leg between gateway and broker, and the loss that matters on that leg is a connection drop, which is not modelled.
  • Over TCP the packet-level model does not apply: retries are TCP's job, and MQTT itself resends only after a reconnect. The numbers describe UDP-style links such as MQTT-SN, not a typical MQTT 3.1.1 client on Wi-Fi.
  • Duplicate handling follows the assumptions in Section 2: the QoS 1 receiver hands every received PUBLISH to the application, and the QoS 2 receiver hands over on the first PUBLISH. Implementations differ, and the second method in the specification delivers on PUBREL instead; the script does not model it, nor the receiver state that stays allocated when the sender aborts QoS 2 after the first phase.
  • One burst configuration (0.25 probability of Bad, loss 0.8, no loss in Good), with the state redrawn per attempt, not a continuous-time process; the delay and \(T_\text{retry}\) are not part of the burst model. The conclusions about \(\rho\) are therefore qualitative; the real value of \(\rho\) depends on how long bursts last compared with \(T_\text{retry}\) and was not measured on a real link.
  • Simulation only. No measurement on a device or radio, and energy is not computed; the packet counts are a proxy and ignore packet length and listening time.

11. Practical conclusions

  1. Pick the transport first. Over TCP, MQTT retransmits only after a reconnect (3.1.1 requires it with CleanSession = 0; 5.0 forbids it otherwise), so QoS 1 and 2 are about surviving connection drops. The formulas above apply to UDP-style links, where the retry timer and counter are part of the protocol.
  2. On such a link at 10% loss, four attempts reduce a lost message from 10% (QoS 0) to 0.009%; at 30% loss the same four attempts still leave 0.81%.
  3. Do not treat a missing PUBACK as proof of loss: at 30% loss, 88% of QoS 1 “failures” were delivered messages whose acknowledgement was lost. Either make the consumer idempotent or accept duplicates.
  4. QoS 1 delivers 1.23 copies per message at 20% loss (19.5% of messages arrive at least twice); if the consumer is not idempotent, either deduplicate by sequence number or pay about twice the packets for QoS 2 (5.49 against 2.77 at 20%).
  5. Size the retry count from \((1-q)^A\le\varepsilon\), not from the default: 0.1% needs 5 attempts at 10% loss and 11 at 30% loss, and each attempt costs a full timer.
  6. If losses are bursty, extra retries spaced closer than the bursts add little (at \(\rho=0.95\), 20 attempts still leave 0.14% undelivered, against \(10^{-14}\) for independent losses); the independent-loss formula is optimistic. Measure burst length on the real link before choosing \(T_\text{retry}\) and \(N_\text{retry}\).
